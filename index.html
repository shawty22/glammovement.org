How global local AI works

Run it close. Share what works.

AI can run on your device or on a group of computers your community shares. That group is a local cluster. Communities can share tools and lessons worldwide while deciding for themselves where their work runs.

Start close to home.

Use smaller models for everyday work, like editing a letter or organizing notes. Pool the hardware so access doesn’t depend on buying a powerful machine.

Reuse good work.

When neighbors ask the same public question, keep a checked answer and its source nearby. Refresh it when the facts change. Keep personal requests separate.

Bring in the cloud when it helps.

Use a larger outside model when the job needs it and the data can go there. Keep basic services available when the connection goes down.

Ten actions for the Mobilization

The GLAMifesto

We’re a technologist-led movement. We build tools with communities so more people can use AI, learn how it works and decide how it’s used. Each commitment includes a proposed test, sources and organizations doing related work.

Planet

01

Protect our water, land and power bills.

Run everyday AI on shared computers in buildings we already have when that makes sense. Before a data center gets a permit or a tax break, make its owners show how much water, land and electricity it will need, and who will pay for power upgrades. Give communities limits they can enforce.

Proposed test

Water, land and electricity. Compare electricity per completed task, peak load, cooling water and the space needed to deliver comparable services. Count the cluster’s idle draw.

SourcesBerkeley Lab energy and water reportVirginia legislative audit

Organizations working on this

  • NAACP A playbook for communities negotiating data-center impacts and protections.
  • Food & Water Watch Bay Area advocacy on data-center siting, water use and energy demand.
02

Build jobs that stay.

Count the jobs that will still be there after a data center opens. Bring libraries, schools, unions and local tech groups together to teach networking, computer repair and how to test AI. Pay local people to run shared computers and help their neighbors use them.

Proposed test

Lasting jobs. Count paid jobs and training hours that remain after installation. Track what residents learn, whether it leads to paid work and how much of the spending stays local.

Virginia’s legislative auditors reported an industry estimate of about 50 full-time workers at a typical 250,000-square-foot data center, roughly half contractors. Construction employs substantially more people.

SourcesVirginia audit on employmentInference Cooperative’s operating model

Organizations working on this

  • Jobs for the Future Registered apprenticeships for AI infrastructure and related trades.
03

Protect our sleep from noise.

Cooling fans can make a steady hum, and backup generators can add bursts of noise. Measure both where people live and sleep. Ask residents how the noise affects their sleep, stress and well-being. Set limits people can enforce and give neighbors someone to call.

Proposed test

Noise. Use a phone app for an initial check. Follow up with a calibrated meter that can measure low-frequency noise. Compare typical sound and peaks at homes, day and night, and invite residents to log sleep disruption and stress.

Virginia’s legislative auditors found estimated levels of 40–59 decibels in selected cases that drew complaints. Those readings describe those cases. WHO links excessive environmental noise to disturbed sleep and reports growing evidence of mental-health effects.

SourcesVirginia audit on siting and noiseWHO guidance on noise and healthNIOSH sound meter app

Organizations working on this

  • Quiet Communities Resources for residents dealing with data-center noise.
04

Use AI without wasting energy.

Treat the electricity AI uses like water at home. Use the smallest tool that does the job well, save useful work so it doesn’t have to be done twice, and turn off idle equipment when it’s safe to do so. Use a bigger model when it makes a real difference.

Proposed test

Everyday use. Compare local and cloud results on writing tasks. Measure accepted answers, retries and energy per accepted result.

SourcesHow People Use ChatGPTRouteLLM routing research

Organizations working on this

  • Mozilla.ai Llamafile packages language models to run on your own computer.
  • Hugging Face Tools and guides for running models locally.

Privacy

05

Keep private life private.

Keep sensitive work on devices or shared computers you trust. Tell people what would leave the system and ask first. Let people see and delete what the service keeps about them, and keep private requests out of shared answer libraries.

Proposed test

Privacy. Test what leaves the system, what other users can see, and what remains after deletion.

SourcesMeanCache researchNIST AI Risk Management Framework

Organizations working on this

  • Electronic Frontier Foundation Practical guidance on what AI tools collect and how to limit exposure.
06

Give people a way to shut AI off.

If AI helps make a decision about you, you should be able to talk to a person and challenge it. If an AI tool behaves dangerously, give operators a way to disconnect it that works independently of the AI itself. Test that essential services can still run safely under human control.

Proposed test

Human control and continuity. In an isolated simulation, test whether an operator can cut an AI system’s command path using a control outside the AI host. Verify that test commands stop reaching the simulated equipment and that the service stays safe under human control.

SourcesHugging Face’s incident and on-premises investigationNIST operational technology security guideARIES Cyber Range

Organizations working on this

  • Idaho National Laboratory Engineering methods for protecting essential functions from cyber disruption.
  • UK AI Security Institute An open benchmark for testing whether AI agents can escape isolated software environments.
07

Protect community knowledge.

Tribal nations decide how their knowledge and data are used. Elders, minority communities and other groups whose knowledge is often overlooked should be able to decide what to pass on, what to keep private, and who may use it. Get permission before recording stories, digitizing materials or using them in AI, and give people a way to withdraw access.

Proposed test

Data sovereignty. Work with willing communities and rights holders. Test who can see, reuse, export or remove material and whether the service honors a decision to withdraw access.

SourcesLocal Contexts Traditional Knowledge LabelsFirst Nations’ OCAP® principlesStoryCorps conversations with elders

Organizations working on this

  • Global Indigenous Data Alliance The CARE Principles for Indigenous authority and governance over data.
  • Te Hiku Media A stewardship license developed for Māori language data.

People

08

Share computers and decide together.

Set up shared computers in places like libraries, schools and cooperatives so people can use local AI without buying expensive equipment. Let the community decide who gets access, what to spend and who runs the service.

Proposed test

Shared access. Compare hardware and track cost per user, wait times, accessibility and who can actually use the service.

SourcesMLCommons client benchmarkInference Cooperative’s member model

Organizations working on this

  • Inference Cooperative A member-governed model for pooling access to AI inference.
  • Public AI A public inference utility exploring shared access to open models.
09

Save answers we can trust.

If fifty neighbors ask when the town picks up bulky waste, check the answer once and save it with the official source. Reuse it when the same public question comes up, and update it when the facts change. Keep personal questions private.

Proposed test

Reuse. Compare exact matches, matches by meaning and reuse of source documents. Count correct hits, stale answers and cloud calls avoided.

SourcesGPTCacheApple’s verified caching research

Organizations working on this

  • Wikimedia Foundation Open knowledge with an emphasis on verifiability and attribution.
  • Creative Commons CC Signals explores how stewards can express preferences for machine reuse.
10

Open the code and show what works.

Use software people can inspect, fix and adapt. Show which AI tools you use, what their licenses allow, when they send work to the cloud, how much they cost and how well they work. Give another community enough information to try it for themselves.

Proposed test

Open evidence. Publish settings, public test tasks and results. Ask another community to repeat the comparison using different hardware.

SourcesOpen Source AI DefinitionInference energy measurement research

Organizations working on this

  • Open Source Initiative A public process for defining what makes an AI system open.
  • EleutherAI An open test harness for comparing language models on common tasks.

Research behind the movement

Put the claims to the test.

The Global Local AI Foundation (GLAF) turns these ten commitments into research questions. Its proposed tests cover what communities can run locally, what work they can safely reuse, and how operators could isolate an AI system while keeping an essential service running.

Why GLAM exists

A movement for people who want to build.

We believe AI can help people make things, learn and solve problems. We also care what it costs in water, electricity and land, and who gets to decide. GLAM brings those questions to the same table.

Global Local AI Mobilization is a technologist-led community movement founded by Ciaran O’Hara. GLAM brings communities and builders together. The Global Local AI Foundation (GLAF) is an exploratory public-interest research project that studies what communities can do with local and hybrid AI.

Get involved

Help Shape a Community Pilot

We’re exploring a pilot around one service that a community wants to run close to home. The pilot design and operating rules are proposals. Hosts, organizers and technical contributors can help decide what to try, who can use it and how to measure whether it works.

Host a pilot.

Bring a library, school, cooperative or other community space that could host a small trial.

Organize locally.

Bring neighbors together to choose a service, propose access rules and tell us what would make it useful.

Build and test.

Help run a node, measure energy or performance, review privacy and access, or teach people how to use the service.
