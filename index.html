The GLAMifesto

These are the commitments we want to put into practice. Each includes an idea, proposed tests, sources and organizations doing related work.

Water, land and power

Protect our water, land and power bills.

The idea

Before a data center gets a permit or a tax break, its owners show how much water, land and electricity it needs and who pays for power upgrades. Communities get limits they can enforce. Where it makes sense, run everyday AI on shared computers in buildings we already have.

Proposed tests

Water, land and electricity. Compare electricity per completed task, peak load, cooling water and the space needed to deliver comparable services. Count the cluster’s idle draw.

Jobs that stay

Train local people to build, run and maintain community AI.

The idea

A typical 250,000-square-foot data center is bigger than four football fields. Industry representatives told Virginia’s auditors it may have about 50 full-time workers, about half of them contractors. A small community cluster can create a different kind of opportunity. Local people can learn to build, run and maintain it, and train in machine learning, networking, computing, inference, AI agents and agentic coding. Each person trained can help train the next. The people who maintain the community system can go on to find good jobs in AI. We want to start with disadvantaged communities.

Proposed tests

Lasting skills and jobs. Count how many local people are trained, how many go on to train others, and whether the training leads to work in AI. Track training hours, what residents learn and how much of the spending stays local.

Virginia’s legislative auditors reported an industry estimate of about 50 full-time workers at a typical 250,000-square-foot data center, roughly half contractors. Construction employs substantially more people.

Quiet homes

Protect people from data center noise, day and night.

The idea

Cooling fans can make a steady low hum, and backup generators can add bursts of noise. Measure both where people live, during the day and at night. Ask residents how the noise affects their daily lives, sleep and well-being. Set limits people can enforce and give neighbors someone to call.

Proposed tests

Noise. Start with a phone app, then use a calibrated meter that can measure low-frequency noise. Compare typical sound and peaks at homes, day and night, and invite residents to log sleep disruption and stress.

Virginia’s legislative auditors found estimated levels of 40–59 decibels in selected cases that drew complaints. Those readings describe those cases. WHO links excessive environmental noise to disturbed sleep and reports growing evidence of mental-health effects.

Energy efficiency

Use AI without wasting energy.

The idea

Don’t send a big request to a distant data center when a small model close to you can do the job. Run everyday work on the smallest model that does it well, on your own device or the local cluster. Save useful work so it isn’t done twice. Let idle machines sleep or power down, for example overnight. Send work to the cloud only when it is needed. This is a thesis we want to test.

Proposed tests

Everyday use. Compare local and cloud results on writing tasks. Measure accepted answers, retries and energy per accepted result.

Organizations
  • Mozilla.ai Llamafile packages language models to run on your own computer.
  • Hugging Face Tools and guides for running models locally.

Privacy

Keep private life private.

The idea

Your private work stays on your device or a shared computer you trust. If something needs to go to an outside service, you are told first and you choose. You can see and delete what is saved about you, and private questions never go into shared answer libraries.

Proposed tests

Privacy. Test what leaves the system, what other users can see, and what remains after deletion.

SourcesMeanCache researchNIST AI Risk Management Framework
Organizations
  • Electronic Frontier Foundation Practical guidance on what AI tools collect and how to limit exposure.

Human control

Give people a way to shut AI off.

The idea

If an AI system goes rogue or behaves dangerously, people need a way to shut it down that does not depend on the AI itself. This is a hard, complex problem, and it may be beyond what a small community pilot can solve. We want to look into experiments on how to shut down or isolate a misbehaving AI, starting with our own cluster, and to share what we learn. Essential services should keep running safely under human control.

This is technically and computationally very complex.

Proposed tests

Human control and continuity. In an isolated simulation, check that an operator can cut an AI system’s command path with a control outside the AI host, that test commands stop reaching the simulated equipment, and that the service stays safe under human control. This covers our own cluster only, not AI running elsewhere.

Organizations

Community knowledge

Protect what communities choose to keep private.

The idea

Some knowledge should not be fed to big cloud AI. AI can supercharge information gathering, and that can turn extractive: taking a community’s knowledge without consent or benefit, which some call digital colonialism, or watching people who never agreed to it. Communities decide how their knowledge and data are used, what to pass on, what to keep private and who may use it. Ask permission first, keep the work on local computers, and let people withdraw access.

Who needs this protection

  • Tribal nations
  • Conservation and wildlife groups
  • Elders and oral historians
  • Traditional healers, farmers and seed keepers
  • Small language communities
  • Artists, writers and musicians
  • Journalists
  • Dissidents
  • Unions and organizers
  • Clinics, shelters and mutual-aid groups
Proposed tests

Data sovereignty. Work with willing communities and rights holders. Test who can see, reuse, export or remove material and whether the service honors a decision to withdraw access.

Community compute

Make local AI available in public spaces, with the community in charge.

The idea

Make local AI publicly accessible in community spaces, such as libraries, community centers and cooperatives. Offer a powerful standalone AI workstation or shared terminals connected to a local cluster, so people can use local AI without buying expensive equipment. The cluster can sit in the same building or nearby. The community decides who gets access, what to spend and who runs the service.

Proposed tests

Shared access. Compare hardware and track cost per user, wait times, accessibility and who can actually use the service.

Organizations

Trusted answers

Build a shared library of checked answers, backed by sources.

The idea

If fifty neighbors ask when the town picks up bulky waste, check the answer once and save it with the official source. Reuse it when the same public question comes up, and update it when the facts change. Personal questions are never saved to the shared library. Over time, checked answers could grow into a community archive that neighbors can search and share privately.

Proposed tests

Reuse. Compare exact matches, matches by meaning and reuse of source documents. Count correct hits, stale answers and cloud calls avoided. Also try the saved answers as a searchable archive, and check who can see what.

SourcesGPTCacheApple’s verified caching research
Organizations
  • Wikimedia Foundation Open knowledge with an emphasis on verifiability and attribution.
  • Creative Commons CC Signals explores how stewards can express preferences for machine reuse.

Open source

Use tools people can inspect, improve and share.

The idea

Run open-source software on computers in the community, so anyone can inspect, fix and adapt it. Prefer AI models you can download and run yourself, and say plainly how open each one really is: its code, its model weights, its training data and its license. Publish what you use, what it costs and how well it works, so another community can try it too.

Proposed tests

Open evidence. Publish settings, public test tasks and results. Ask another community to repeat the comparison using different hardware.

SourcesOpen Source AI DefinitionInference energy measurement research
Organizations
  • Open Source Initiative A public process for defining what makes an AI system open.
  • EleutherAI An open test harness for comparing language models on common tasks.

Proposed community pilots

Put the ideas to work.

Start with a service a community wants to run. Pool compute so a neighborhood can use its own private AI, store and reuse useful information, and call on the cloud when needed. Test answer quality, privacy, cost, and water and energy use. Explore how close local AI can come to leading frontier models on selected reasoning tasks.

Start close to home.

Use smaller models for everyday work, like editing a letter or organizing notes. Pool the hardware so access doesn’t depend on buying a powerful machine.

Reuse good work.

When neighbors ask the same public question, keep a checked answer and its source nearby. Refresh it when the facts change. Keep personal requests separate.

Bring in the cloud when it helps.

Use a larger outside model when the job needs it and the data can go there. Keep basic services available when the connection goes down.

Putting skills to work

Build skills that can lead to work.

Through education, training and hands-on projects, we aim to prepare participants for technical jobs in AI, networking and computing. We want to test whether those skills lead to paid local work and help people train others.

Why GLAM exists

A movement for people who want to build.

We believe AI can help people make things, learn and solve problems. We also care what it costs in water, electricity and land, and who gets to decide. GLAM brings those questions to the same table.

Global Local AI Mobilization was founded by Ciaran O’Hara, who also founded the Global Local AI Foundation (GLAF). GLAM organizes advocacy and community participation. GLAF explores public-interest research into local and hybrid AI.
